Exercise 03

Meet the hardware security key

A small physical device — like a YubiKey — that proves it's really you, without a code to type or a text message to intercept.

A hardware key plugs into a USB port or taps over NFC. When a site asks for your second factor, you simply touch the key instead of typing a code — nothing can be phished, guessed, or intercepted, because the proof never leaves the device.

Insert the simulated key to see how it completes the circuit.

2FA
Decrypted note

Two-factor authentication turns "something you know" — a password — into "something you know plus something you have." Even if your password leaks in a breach, an attacker still can't get in without physically holding your key. Of the available second-factor methods, hardware keys resist phishing better than SMS codes or one-time codes typed from an app, because the key cryptographically verifies the website itself before it responds.

File 02

Two-Factor Authentication

A password alone is one lock on one door. Two-factor authentication adds a second, physical lock — a code from your phone or a hardware key — so a stolen password by itself opens nothing. Enable it first on your email; everything else recovers through that account.

File 03

Data Privacy

Encrypted data is unreadable without the key, even if it's stolen outright. Before sharing personal information with an app or service, ask what it collects, why, and how long it's kept — the safest data is the data that was never handed over.

Protocol

Three steps to secure your identity

Scroll down — each step confirms itself as it enters view.

01

Lock down your email first

It's the recovery path for almost every other account you own. A long passphrase plus two-factor authentication here protects everything downstream of it.

02

Give every account its own password

A password manager makes this painless. Reused passwords mean one leaked database can unlock accounts on services that were never breached themselves.

03

Slow down before you click

Urgency is the most common tactic in social engineering. A moment's pause to verify a sender or a request stops most attacks before they start.
